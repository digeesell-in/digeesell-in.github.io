# How to Brief a Content Agency in India: Topics, Proof and a Review Process That Protects Lead Quality

A good content brief fixes what each page is for before anyone writes: the buyer question it answers, the language and script it is written in, who checks the facts, how gated forms handle consent, and what the page will be measured against. A vague brief like "four blogs a month" usually gets generic posts and no enquiries.

Whether you are commissioning content for the first time or replacing a writer, use the sections below to build a brief that an agency or freelancer can act on.

## Start with the questions buyers ask, not a word count

Your sales team already hears the questions that matter. Collect them from sales calls, enquiry forms, WhatsApp chats and proposal meetings. For example:

- "What does this cost for a company our size?"
- "How long does implementation take?"
- "Do you work with businesses in our city?"

Then apply one rule: **one intent, one page**. Assign each question to a single page so your own pages don't compete with each other in search. Word count follows from the question, not the other way round.

## Say which languages and scripts matter

### English, Hindi and regional scripts

The same question is searched differently in English, Hindi and regional languages, and the results that come back differ too. Tell the agency which languages your buyers actually use and ask for research per language, not one keyword list translated three times.

### Native writers, not translated drafts

Regional-language pages should be written by native writers and published on their own URLs, with hreflang tags connecting the versions. A translated English draft rarely reads naturally.

Transliterated Hinglish is different. It usually works best used naturally on the English or Hindi page, not as a separate page of its own.

### Voice questions

People who ask by voice tend to phrase full questions. Treat this as good practice rather than a statistic, and ask writers to:

- answer the question in a short sentence near the start,
- use question-form headings,
- state locality, landmarks and timings as plain text, not only inside images or maps.

## Put proof and expert review in the brief

Specialist claims need a specialist to check them. Your brief should name who that is (a doctor, a chartered accountant, an engineer) and schedule their review before publication, not after.

Also ask the agency directly:

- Do you use AI assistance in drafting, and do you disclose it?
- What editorial policy are drafts edited and reviewed under?
- How will claims be substantiated?

Claims in marketing content should be backed by evidence, in line with the ASCI code. Keep this point in the brief so it isn't left to chance.

## Gated content needs DPDP-ready forms

If a page offers a download, checklist or webinar in exchange for details, the form matters as much as the content. Under the DPDP Act 2023, build the form so that it:

- collects only what you need,
- gives a clear notice of the purpose,
- asks for consent as a separate action, never a pre-ticked box,
- asks separately for consent to marketing follow-up,
- stores consent records with the lead in your CRM.

This is general information, not legal advice. Have the consent wording approved by your legal adviser.

## Agree what each page will be measured against

Pageviews are easy to report and easy to inflate. Report them, but don't treat them as the result. Agree measures that connect to business, such as:

| Measure | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Indexation and rankings per language | Shows each language version is actually found |
| Onward clicks to service pages | Shows readers moving towards a decision |
| Qualified enquiries | Shows whether content brings the right leads |

Set these before work begins so neither side argues about success later.

## A one-page brief template

Copy this for every page you commission:

- **Goal and audience:** who the page is for and what it should lead them to do.
- **Buyer question:** the one question this page answers.
- **Language and script:** English, Hindi, a regional language, or more than one.
- **Must-include facts and proof:** what must be stated, and the evidence behind it.
- **Reviewer:** the named expert who checks claims, and when.
- **CTA:** the single next step for the reader.
- **Success measure:** the one or two numbers you will judge it on.
- **Deadline:** including time for expert review.

## Why this protects lead quality

A brief built this way keeps content tied to real buyer questions, accurate in every language, reviewed before it goes live and judged on enquiries rather than traffic. That is what separates content that brings qualified leads from content that only fills a blog.
